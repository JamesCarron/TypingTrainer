Five different answers to the same question, not five skins of one answer. 01Reader

The book is the interface. You are not taking 444 tests, you are reading The Art of War with your hands. The page shows the surrounding prose set like a book, dimmed; the line you are on sits in place within it, and the page turns as you go. Progress is spatial rather than numeric — the rail on the right is the edge of the book.

Why it might winYou are typing a book, and no competitor treats it as one. Context above and below makes the prose make sense, which is the actual pleasure of this over Monkeytype.
What it costsA serif page and a monospace live line is a jarring mix, and the surrounding prose competes with the line you are on. Numbers have nowhere to live, so criteria and WPM move out of sight entirely.

02Cockpit

The opposite bet: you are practising deliberately, so show the instruments while you fly. Live WPM and accuracy on the left, the next key and your error heat on a keyboard to the right, and a rhythm trace along the bottom that scrolls as you type — the flat stretches are your fluent runs, the spikes are the hesitations the analysis later calls stalls.

03Coach

The session is a workout, and the app decides what it contains. Warm up on the keys you are worst at, then the book, then a review of what changed. One thing on screen at a time, and the reasoning always visible — the drill is not “here is a line”, it is “quotes are your worst category at 5%, so here are thirty seconds of them”. The analysis stops being a page you might visit and becomes the thing driving the session.

Why it might winIt is the only direction that closes the loop by itself. Everything already built — targets, drills, hard lines, adaptive criteria — has somewhere obvious to appear, and you never have to decide what to practise.
What it costsIt takes the wheel. On a day you only want to read the next page of the book, a five-step plan is an obstacle, so the escape hatch has to be genuinely one key.

04Terminal

Built for the hands, not the mouse. A modal, keyboard-driven interface in the vim idiom: a status line that always tells you where you are, a command line for everything else, and no control you have to reach for a pointer to hit. :jump 347, :drill quote, :stats, :set wpm 65. The audience is people who care enough about typing to practise it daily; they are the same people who already live like this.

What it costsDiscoverability: nothing is visible until you know it exists, so :help has to be excellent. Monospace everything makes the prose harder to read than it needs to be, and it is a style, not to everyone's taste.

05Zen

Strip further than Monkeytype does. One line, centred, large. No counters, no criteria, no upcoming text, no buttons — nothing that invites a glance away. Neighbouring lines are barely-there ghosts so you know the shape of what is coming without reading it. Every number waits until you stop; the dots are the only concession, and they are deliberately imprecise.

Why it might winFlow. Nothing competes with the sentence, so accuracy tends to rise on its own, and the payoff lands where it belongs — in the session summary, once you have stopped.
What it costsIt hides things you sometimes genuinely need mid-session: which criteria are active, how far the line is from passing, whether stop-on-error is on. And with no live feedback, a run of failures can feel arbitrary.
